Inside a data center: the building the cloud lives in

Data center operations is a common bridge job into IT, especially from an electrical background. Here you learn the power chains, redundancy, cooling, racks and the procedures that keep it all running.

Warm-up

Part 1 · The server lives in one of these

The game server runs in an Oracle data center in Singapore. Metro Manila has its own: the big STT campus being built in Fairview, Quezon City (planned 124 MW), plus ePLDT VITRO, Converge and others. A data center is a building designed for one job: keep thousands of computers powered, cool and connected, every second of every year.

The people who keep it running are data center technicians and facilities/critical-environment technicians. Their work: rack and stack (installing servers), cabling, “remote hands” tasks for customers, monitoring alarms, preventive maintenance, and following written procedures for every change.

Part 2 · The power chain

  • When Meralco power fails, the UPS batteries carry the load for minutes while the generator starts and the ATS switches over. Servers never notice.
  • Servers have two power supplies. Each rack gets an A feed and a B feed from separate UPS systems, so one whole side can fail or be shut down for maintenance and nothing goes off.
  • A device with only one power cord is a single point of failure (SPOF): one fault takes it down. Technicians flag these. A static transfer switch (STS) can feed a single-corded device from two sources.

Redundancy words you'll hear in every interview:

WordMeaning
NExactly enough capacity. No spare.
N+1One extra unit (e.g., 4 UPS modules needed, 5 installed).
2NTwo complete, independent systems (A and B). Either can carry everything.

The Uptime Institute's Tier levels describe the whole site: Tier I basic · Tier II redundant components · Tier III concurrently maintainable (any part can be maintained without shutting down IT) · Tier IV fault tolerant (survives any single failure automatically).

Part 3 · Cooling, and the alarms that come with it

Every watt of electricity a server uses becomes heat. Remove the heat or the servers shut themselves down.

  • Racks face each other in rows: cold aisle (front, where servers breathe in) and hot aisle (back, where hot air comes out). Mixing them wastes cooling. That's why blanking panels fill empty rack spaces.
  • CRAC/CRAH units or precision air conditioning units (PACU in the Converge ad) cool the room; chillers cool the water for big sites.
  • ASHRAE's recommended server inlet range is 18–27 °C. Alarms trigger outside that range.
  • A BMS (building management system) watches power and cooling equipment and raises alarms. DCIM software adds rack-level detail.
  • PUE (power usage effectiveness) = total facility power ÷ IT power. 1,500 kW total for 1,000 kW of IT = PUE 1.5. Lower is better. 1.0 would mean zero overhead.

Part 4 · Racks, cabling and procedures

  • Rack space is counted in U: 1U = 1.75 inches (44.45 mm). A standard rack is 42U.
  • Copper: Cat6/Cat6A. The structured cabling standard (TIA-568) limits a copper channel to 100 m: 90 m of permanent link plus 10 m of patch cords. Fiber (single-mode for long runs, multimode for short) uses LC connectors inside the building.
  • Labels on both ends of every cable, matching the drawings.
  • Nothing changes without a written procedure: an MOP (method of procedure) for planned work, SOPs for routine tasks, and EOPs (emergency operating procedures) for failures. Work happens in approved change windows.
  • Safety: lock-out/tag-out (LOTO) before electrical work, ESD straps for electronics, escorts and access logs.

Part 5 · A calculation they might give you

A rack has 10 servers at 450 W each, on a 230 V single-phase rack PDU rated 32 A.

  • Load: 10 × 450 = 4,500 W. Current: 4,500 ÷ 230 ≈ 19.6 A.
  • Continuous-load headroom: 32 A × 0.8 = 25.6 A available. 19.6 A fits.
  • But with A/B feeds, each PDU must be able to carry the WHOLE rack if the other side fails. So each side must handle 19.6 A, not half of it. Many beginners split the load in half and get this wrong.
Say it yourself first, then open this

“Power goes utility, switchgear, ATS with generator, UPS, PDU, rack PDU, dual power supplies. A and B feeds mean either side can carry the full load, which is 2N. N+1 means one spare unit. Cooling uses hot and cold aisles and alarms if inlet temperature leaves 18–27 °C. Every change follows an MOP.”

Practice 2 · Draw it

30 minutes.

  • In app.diagrams.net, draw a one-line diagram for a small data hall: utility, transformer, ATS + generator, two UPS (A and B), two PDUs, three racks. Each rack gets an A and a B feed.
  • Add one single-corded device on purpose, and mark it in red as a SPOF.
  • Write 5 lines under the drawing: what happens when (1) Meralco fails, (2) UPS-A is taken down for maintenance, (3) the generator fails to start, (4) PDU-B trips, (5) the red device's feed fails. Check yourself with the simulator in Sheet 3.

PASS/FAIL CHECK

  • Every rack traces back to two independent UPS systems, except the one SPOF you marked.
  • Your 5 “what happens” lines are correct (check them with someone who works in a data center, or an AI tutor).
  • A working data center or facilities technician has looked at it (LinkedIn message, or r/datacenter) and you've written their comments in your progress notes.
