NOC work: alarms, tickets and calm nights

Monitoring is the most-named skill across all 68 ads. You'll learn how monitoring tools see devices, the incident life cycle, the words every NOC uses, and how to write a ticket someone can pick up at 3 AM.

Why this lesson

NOC ads ask for incident handling, ticketing and tools like Zabbix or PRTG. Monitoring is named in data center, NOC and cloud ads alike.

Warm-up

Part 1 · What a NOC does

A NOC (network operations center) is a room (or a remote team) watching screens 24/7 for many customers' networks. When something breaks, the NOC is first to know, and their job is to restore service fast and get the right people involved. The data center equivalent watches power and cooling.

A small game server with a monitor is already a mini-NOC: the monitor collects metrics, sends phone alerts, and auto-restarts on low memory.

Part 2 · How monitoring tools see devices

  • Ping (ICMP): “are you alive?” The simplest check.
  • SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol): the monitoring server polls devices for numbers like traffic, temperature and errors on UDP 161. Devices can also push urgent messages called traps on UDP 162.
  • Syslog: devices send their log messages to a central server, usually on UDP 514.
  • Port checks: “does port 22 answer?” (like Test-NetConnection in Lesson 01).

Common tools: Zabbix and PRTG (both named in Philippine NOC ads), plus Grafana for dashboards.

Part 3 · The incident life cycle

Severity, typical meaning: P1 critical: service down for many users · P2 high: badly degraded · P3 medium: one user or a workaround exists · P4 low: questions, small requests.

ITIL words every NOC uses (ITIL is the standard IT service management framework):

  • Incident: something broke; restore service as fast as possible.
  • Problem: the underlying cause of one or more incidents; fix it so it doesn't come back.
  • Change: a planned modification, approved before it happens (like an MOP in a data center).
  • SLA: the promised response or restore time. MTTR: mean time to restore.

CompTIA's 7-step troubleshooting method (memorise it for Network+). Your Lesson 06 “five checkpoints” is steps 2–3 in action.

Part 4 · Writing a ticket people can use at 3 AM

Ticket template: Ticket ID · Opened (date/time) · Source (alert or person) · Severity · Affected service · Symptom (what is seen, exactly) · Impact (who and how many) · Timeline of actions (each line timestamped: what you checked, what you saw) · Escalated to (who, when) · Resolution · Closed (date/time) · Root cause (if known) · Follow-up needed.

Good ticket line: “02:14 Ping to 10.30.0.10 failing from NOC; R2 172.16.0.2 reachable. Suspect server-side or return route. Checking R2 routing table.” Bad ticket line: “Server down, checking.”

Practice 2 · Build a mini-NOC on your laptop

30 minutes. PRTG's free edition (up to 100 sensors) runs on Windows. If it's no longer offered when you read this, use Uptime Kuma or Zabbix instead. The steps are the same idea.

  • laptopInstall PRTG. Add a ping sensor for your home router (your default gateway from ipconfig).
  • laptopAdd a ping sensor for 8.8.8.8 (the internet).
  • laptopAdd a port sensor: the server's public IP, TCP port 22 (is SSH answering?).
  • laptopAdd a ping sensor for your phone's Wi-Fi IP address (find it in the phone's Wi-Fi settings).
  • laptopWatch them turn green. This is your “NOC screen”.

PASS/FAIL CHECK · the incident drill

  • laptopTurn your phone's Wi-Fi off. Note the time.
  • laptopWhen sensor 4 turns red, acknowledge it in PRTG. Note how many minutes it took to alert.
  • Write a full ticket using the template (treat the phone as “the CCTV recorder”).
  • laptopTurn Wi-Fi back on. Verify green. Close the ticket.
  • Write a one-page postmortem for a REAL game-server incident (a memory restart or crash from the monitor's history): summary, impact, timeline, root cause, what went well, what to improve, action items with dates. Keep it blameless: describe systems and decisions, not people's faults.
PASS

Ticket and postmortem done within 30 minutes of the alert, and every timeline line has a time and a fact.
